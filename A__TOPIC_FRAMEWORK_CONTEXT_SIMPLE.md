# Topic Modeling Framework: Context & Rationale (Simple Topics)

## Project Overview
**Research Question**: How is the legacy of Slavery addressed through developmental policies in the Dutch Caribbean during the International Decade for the People of African Descent (IDPAD, 2015-2024)?

**Methodology**: Dictionary-based topic modeling using a **categorical approach** that identifies four fundamental dimensions of slavery-related discourse in policy documents.

---

## The 4-Topic Framework: Core Categories of Slavery Discourse

This research uses **4 primary topics** that represent distinct but interconnected dimensions of how slavery and its legacies appear in policy texts. Unlike problem-oriented frameworks, these topics are **categorical** - they identify the domain of discussion rather than specific developmental challenges.

The framework emerges from:
1. **UN IDPAD priorities** (2015-2024)
2. **Historical scholarship** on Dutch colonial slavery (Nimako & Willemsen 2011; Staat en Slavernij 2023; Ketenen van het Verleden 2021)
3. **Memory studies** and commemoration practices (Wekker 2016; Kardux 2020)
4. **Critical race theory** and contemporary racism research (Essed 1991; Wekker 2016)

---

## Topic 1: Colonial Systems

### What This Topic Captures
Policy discourse that discusses **colonial institutions, governance structures, and economic systems** established under Dutch colonialism, including:

- Colonial administrative structures (governorships, colonial councils, etc.)
- Economic systems (plantations, trade monopolies, extraction economies)
- The West India Company (WIC) and similar institutions
- Colonial territories (Suriname, Antilles, Caribbean colonies)
- Decolonization processes and constitutional changes
- Colonial-era legal frameworks and their remnants

### WHY This Topic Matters
Colonial systems were the **structural framework** through which slavery operated. Policy that references colonial governance, plantation economies, or decolonization is addressing the institutional legacy of slavery even when not discussing enslaved people directly.

### Key Historical Context
- **Dutch West India Company (WIC)**: Established 1621, controlled trade and governance in Dutch colonies
- **Plantation colonies**: Suriname, Curaçao, and others developed as extraction economies
- **Colonial governance**: Governors appointed from Netherlands; local populations excluded from power
- **Decolonization**: Suriname independence (1975); Antilles constitutional reforms (2010); ongoing debates about colonial relationships

### Policy Text Examples
- "The governance structure inherited from the colonial period..."
- "Plantation economy dependency continues to shape..."
- "Following decolonization efforts..."
- "The West India Company's legacy in..."
- "Colonial-era institutions that still..."

### Related Historical Events/Periods
- Establishment of WIC (1621)
- Peak plantation era (1700s-1800s)
- Suriname independence (1975)
- Netherlands Antilles dissolution (2010)
- Ongoing constitutional debates about autonomy

## Topic 2: Historical Slavery

### What This Topic Captures
Policy discourse that directly discusses **the historical practice of chattel slavery**, including:

- The institution of slavery itself
- Enslaved people and their experiences
- Slave trade (trans-Atlantic, domestic)
- Slavery practices (forced labor, violence, family separation)
- Resistance and rebellion (maroons, uprisings)
- Abolition and emancipation (1863 in Dutch colonies)
- Slave owners, traders, and their roles

### WHY This Topic Matters
This is the **historical core** of the slavery legacy. Policy that references the actual practice of slavery—the violence, exploitation, and human suffering—is directly engaging with the foundational historical injustice.

### Key Historical Context
- **Dutch involvement in slave trade**: 500,000+ people enslaved and transported
- **Chattel slavery system**: People treated as property; complete denial of human rights
- **Violence and brutality**: Whips, chains, punishments; family separations
- **Resistance**: Maroon communities (escaped enslaved people), slave revolts (1795 Curaçao, 1763 Berbice)
- **Middle Passage**: Horrific transatlantic journey from Africa to Caribbean
- **Abolition**: 1863 in Dutch colonies (with 10-year "transition" period of continued forced labor)
- **No reparations**: Enslaved people received nothing; some slave owners were compensated

### Policy Text Examples
- "The abolition of slavery in 1863..."
- "Enslaved people who were forcibly brought..."
- "The trans-Atlantic slave trade..."
- "Descendants of enslaved Africans..."
- "Resistance by maroons and rebel enslaved people..."
- "The brutal conditions on plantations..."

### Related Historical Events/Periods
- Dutch entry into slave trade (1600s)
- Peak of Dutch slave trade (1700s)
- Slave rebellions (1763 Berbice, 1795 Curaçao, 1863 Suriname)
- Abolition/Emancipation (1863)
- Post-emancipation labor contracts (1863-1873)

## Topic 3: Heritage & Memory

### What This Topic Captures
Policy discourse about **how slavery is commemorated, remembered, taught, and preserved** in contemporary society, including:

- Commemorative events (Keti Koti, July 1st ceremonies)
- Monuments, memorials, and museums
- History education and awareness programs
- Recognition and acknowledgment of slavery's history
- Storytelling, testimonies, and oral histories
- Cultural heritage preservation
- Descendants and diaspora communities
- National apologies and symbolic gestures

### WHY This Topic Matters
Memory and heritage work is **crucial for reparative justice**. How societies remember slavery determines whether its legacies are acknowledged, taught to new generations, and addressed in policy. This topic captures the "Satisfaction" dimension of UN reparations (non-monetary measures like apologies, memorials, truth-telling).

### Key Historical Context
- **Keti Koti**: "Breaking the Chains" - July 1st commemoration of 1863 abolition
- **Slavery monuments**: National Monument Amsterdam (2002); local monuments in Caribbean
- **Recognition timeline**: Netherlands formal apology (2023); growing acknowledgment since 2000s
- **Education gaps**: Slavery history often minimized or absent from Dutch curriculum until recently
- **Diaspora**: Caribbean Dutch communities maintaining cultural memory across generations

### Policy Text Examples
- "Keti Koti commemoration activities..."
- "The slavery monument serves as..."
- "History education about the slavery past..."
- "Recognition of descendants' experiences..."
- "Museum exhibitions on colonial history..."
- "Preserving cultural heritage of enslaved communities..."
- "National apology for slavery..."

### Related Events/Initiatives
- Keti Koti national holiday establishment
- National Slavery Monument (Amsterdam, 2002)
- Slavery apology (December 2023)
- UNESCO Slave Route Project
- History education curriculum reforms
- Community heritage projects

## Topic 4: Modern Racism & Discrimination

### What This Topic Captures
Policy discourse about **contemporary racial inequality and discrimination** that connects to or results from slavery's legacy, including:

- Racism and discrimination (structural, institutional, interpersonal)
- Racial inequality and segregation
- Colorism and skin color hierarchies
- Stereotyping and prejudice
- Exclusion and marginalization
- Everyday racism and microaggressions
- Anti-racism initiatives and activism
- Inclusion and equality efforts
- Safety, belonging, and citizenship for racialized communities

### WHY This Topic Matters
Slavery created **racial hierarchies that persist today**. Modern racism is not a separate issue from slavery—it's a direct continuation of the racial categories, hierarchies, and ideologies that justified slavery. Policy addressing contemporary racism without acknowledging its roots in slavery misses the structural nature of the problem.

### Key Historical Context
- **Racial hierarchies under slavery**: White masters > mixed-race "free coloreds" > enslaved Blacks
- **Colorism**: Lighter skin privileged; darker skin oppressed (persists today)
- **Scientific racism**: Colonial-era theories of racial inferiority embedded in institutions
- **Post-slavery discrimination**: Segregation, employment discrimination, housing discrimination continued after abolition
- **Everyday racism** (Philomena Essed 1991): Routine discriminatory experiences in daily life
- **Structural racism**: Racial inequalities embedded in institutions (education, employment, housing, criminal justice)

### Policy Text Examples
- "Addressing structural racism in..."
- "Discrimination based on ethnic background..."
- "Reducing racial inequality in..."
- "Anti-racism education and training..."
- "Marginalized communities facing..."
- "Colorism within Caribbean communities..."
- "Safe and inclusive environment for Black Dutch..."
- "Combating everyday racism and microaggressions..."

### Contemporary Manifestations
- Employment discrimination
- Housing segregation
- Educational racism (tracking, exclusion)
- Police profiling
- Health disparities
- Media stereotyping
- Political underrepresentation

## Cross-Cutting Themes

### 1. Interconnection Between Topics
These four topics are **not isolated** - they frequently overlap:

- **Colonial Systems** established the framework for **Historical Slavery**
- **Historical Slavery** created racial ideologies that fuel **Modern Racism**
- **Heritage & Memory** work is essential for addressing both history and contemporary inequality
- **Modern Racism** exists in institutions inherited from **Colonial Systems**

### 2. Temporal Dimensions
The topics span different time periods:

- **Historical Slavery**: 1600s-1863 (primary focus)
- **Colonial Systems**: 1600s-1975/2010 (some structures persist)
- **Heritage & Memory**: 1863-present (intensified since 2000s)
- **Modern Racism**: 1863-present (ongoing)

### 3. From Silence to Recognition (2000s-2020s)
Dutch society has undergone significant shifts in discussing slavery:

- **Pre-2000s**: Minimal public discussion; slavery treated as distant history
- **2000s**: Growing commemoration (National Monument 2002; Keti Koti awareness)
- **2010s**: Academic research intensifies (Wekker 2016; Nimako); Black Pete debates
- **2020s**: Black Lives Matter (2020); slavery apology (2023); reparations demands

## How These Topics Relate to Reparative Justice

### UN Reparation Measures Framework
The 4 topics map onto UN reparation categories:

1. **Cessation** (stop ongoing harm) → **Modern Racism & Discrimination** topic
2. **Restitution** (restore what was taken) → **Colonial Systems** + **Historical Slavery** topics
3. **Rehabilitation** (services for recovery) → All topics
4. **Compensation** (financial payments) → **Historical Slavery** topic
5. **Satisfaction** (non-monetary measures) → **Heritage & Memory** topic

### Policy Alignment Questions
When analyzing policy texts:

- Does it acknowledge the **historical context** (Colonial Systems + Historical Slavery)?
- Does it engage with **memory and recognition** (Heritage & Memory)?
- Does it address **contemporary impacts** (Modern Racism)?
- Does it take a **reparative approach** (victim-centered, structural, restorative)?

## Why This Framework Matters for Text Analysis

### Categorical vs. Problem-Oriented Approach
This framework uses **categorical topics** rather than specific problems:

- **Advantage**: Easier to distinguish - text is either about historical slavery or contemporary racism; about colonial governance or memory work
- **Tradeoff**: Less granular - doesn't distinguish between educational vs. economic problems
- **Purpose**: Captures **how policy engages with slavery** (historically, institutionally, commemoratively, or in terms of ongoing racial inequality)

### Evaluation Questions to Ask
When determining which topic(s) a policy text addresses:

1. **Is it discussing colonial-era institutions/governance?** → Colonial Systems
2. **Is it discussing the historical practice of slavery itself?** → Historical Slavery
3. **Is it about commemoration, memory, education, recognition?** → Heritage & Memory
4. **Is it about contemporary racism and discrimination?** → Modern Racism & Discrimination

### Multiple Topics Are Common
Many policy texts will address **multiple topics simultaneously**:

- Example: A policy on slavery education addresses both **Historical Slavery** (content) and **Heritage & Memory** (educational purpose)
- Example: Anti-racism policy might reference **Historical Slavery** (root cause) while addressing **Modern Racism** (current problem)
